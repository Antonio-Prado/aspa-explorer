False positives

When the ASPAs prove a route Invalid, is it a leak, or an ASPA that forgot a provider? Measured on the routes of a route collector and on what the networks did with their ASPAs afterwards.

The question

The verification of an AS path is only as right as the ASPAs it reads. An ASPA that leaves out one of the providers of its network makes the routes that come through that provider Invalid, with no leak anywhere: a false positive (see Forgetting a provider). Counting the Invalid routes says how many there are, not how many of them are mistakes of this kind. This page tries to tell them apart, and takes as proof the one thing that only the network can say: whether it later added that provider to its ASPA.

How it is measured

Every route of the table dump of 00:00 UTC of a RIPE RIS collector, for each day from – to –, is checked with the downstream procedure against the ASPAs as they were at the start of that day. The Invalid routes are grouped by their place: the AS where the climb from the origin stops, the ASes in between, and the AS where the descent to the collector stops. At both of those ends an ASPA misses the AS next to it; that pair, the customer and the AS that its ASPA misses, is the failing pair.

Each place is then followed for 30 days in the history of the ASPAs. When its paths stop being Invalid because the customer added exactly the AS it was missing, the network has said itself that the AS is one of its providers: a false positive confirmed by the operator. The other places are sorted by what can be said of them, first that applies:

  • Fixed: the missing provider was added within the window.
  • ASPA withdrawn: an ASPA of the path was withdrawn and the paths were no longer Invalid.
  • Structural: the failing pair belongs to the same organisation (CAIDA AS Organizations), or the AS in between is a DDoS scrubbing network (a short list by hand).
  • Probable false positive: CAIDA or the IRR (the aut-num of either side) says that the AS in between is a transit provider of the customer, and the place lasted more than two days or was seen by three feeders or more.
  • Undetermined: everything else, including the short ones without evidence. A dump of 00:00 cannot tell a short leak from a gap fixed the next day, and most short places turned out to be the latter.

The code is in tools/false-positives of the repository, and the figures on this page in /data/false-positives.json. No AS number is published here.

The route leaks of the detector

The table dumps miss the leaks that last minutes. The leaks that the detector of the Incidents page finds in the updates, and that the ASPAs of the day prove Invalid, are sorted in the same way, with one difference: there a leak with no evidence of transit at either end, and not a persistent valley, is a probable leak. Only the leaks whose three ASes (where it came from, the AS that leaked, where it went) are Invalid by themselves can be followed; for the others the Invalid comes from elsewhere in the path.

What this cannot say

  • A place that nobody fixed is not shown to be right: most long-lived places with evidence of transit were never fixed either. The fixes are a lower bound of the false positives, not their count.
  • One collector sees what its feeders send it. When the ASPA that misses a provider is the one of a feeder itself, all the routes of that feeder are Invalid at once: counts of routes say more about the feeders than about the Internet.
  • The history of the ASPAs keeps the last state of each day: an ASPA published and fixed on the same day is never seen.
  • CAIDA infers its relationships from the same kind of paths, so it is not an independent witness for a place that lasts; the IRR objects are today's, as the registries keep no history; the ASes of the exchange points are left out of the paths, so a route server that adds its own AS cannot show up as a cause; the list of scrubbing networks is short and by hand.
