False positives

When the ASPAs prove a route Invalid, is it a leak, or an ASPA that forgot a provider? Measured on the routes of a route collector and on what the networks did with their ASPAs afterwards.

The question

The verification of an AS path is only as right as the ASPAs it reads. An ASPA that leaves out one of the providers of its network makes the routes that come through that provider Invalid, with no leak anywhere: a false positive (see Forgetting a provider). Counting the Invalid routes says how many there are, not how many of them are mistakes of this kind. This page tries to tell them apart, using the one thing that only the network can say: whether it later added that AS to its ASPA.

How it is measured

Every route of the table dump of 00:00 UTC of a RIPE RIS collector, for each day from – to –, is checked with the downstream procedure. Until 15 October 2026 the history of the ASPAs comes from the daily archive of the RIPE NCC: one state a day, taken between 04:00 and 06:00 UTC in this period, and none of midnight. The dump falls some 19 hours after one snapshot and some 5 before the next. So each route is checked twice, with the ASPAs of the snapshot before the dump and with those of the snapshot after it, and it is counted only when it is Invalid with both: a route that is Invalid with one alone owes it to an ASPA that changed in those 24 hours, and what that ASPA said at midnight is not known. From 17 October the history comes from the job of this site, once a day at first (at 04:33 UTC, like the archive) and every hour from the afternoon of 19 October: from the 20th the two states around midnight are an hour apart at most, or the same. The dump of 16 October, which falls between the two sources, is left out. The Invalid routes are grouped by their place: the AS where the climb from the origin stops, the ASes in between, and the AS where the descent to the collector stops. At each of those two ends an ASPA does not list the AS next to it. That is no mistake in itself, since a network does not list its customers and peers; the question is whether one of the two is in fact a provider that the ASPA forgot.

Each place is then followed for 30 days in the history of the ASPAs. When its paths stop being Invalid because the customer added exactly the AS it was missing, the network has said itself that it counts that AS among its providers. That is the closest thing to a confirmation that the Invalid was a false positive: it is what the operator says, not a proof that the AS is a transit provider. The other places are sorted by what can be said of them, first that applies:

  • Fixed: the missing AS was added within the window.
  • ASPA withdrawn: an ASPA of the path was withdrawn and the paths were no longer Invalid.
  • Structural: the two ASes at one of the ends belong to the same organisation (CAIDA AS Organizations), or the AS in between is a DDoS scrubbing network (a short list by hand).
  • Probable false positive: CAIDA or the IRR (the aut-num of either side) says that the AS in between is a transit provider of the customer, and the place lasted more than two days or was seen by three feeders or more.
  • Undetermined: everything else, including the short ones without evidence: a dump of 00:00 cannot tell a short leak from a gap that was soon fixed.

The first version of this page, of 25 November 2026, checked each dump only with the snapshot before it: the routes whose ASPA had been completed in those 19 hours counted as Invalid and then as fixed the next day, and the share of the places fixed by the operator came out at more than twice the one here.

The code is in tools/false-positives of the repository, and the figures on this page in /data/false-positives.json. No AS number is published here.

What this cannot say

  • A place that nobody fixed is not shown to be right: most long-lived places with evidence of transit were never fixed either. The fixes are a lower bound of the false positives, not their count.
  • One collector sees what its feeders send it, and most places are seen by a single feeder: the counts of routes say as much about the feeders as about the Internet.
  • For the days of the archive the history of the ASPAs has one state a day: a gap that opened and closed between two snapshots is never seen, and a fix on day 1 means that the provider was in the next daily snapshot, some hours or a day later.
  • The route leaks that last minutes are not in a dump of 00:00: this page is about the Invalid routes that stay in the tables.
  • CAIDA infers its relationships from the same kind of paths, so it is not an independent witness for a place that lasts; the IRR objects are today's, as the registries keep no history; the ASes of the exchange points are left out of the paths, so a route server that adds its own AS cannot show up as a cause; the list of scrubbing networks is short and by hand.
