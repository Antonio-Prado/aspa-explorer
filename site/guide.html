What you are looking at

An ASPA (Autonomous System Provider Authorization) is a signed object published in the RPKI. In it, a customer AS lists the ASes that are allowed to act as its upstream providers. Routers that validate AS paths use these lists to spot route leaks and some forged paths.

ASPA Explorer shows every ASPA that a Routinator validator currently considers valid. Nothing here is edited or interpreted: the page reads the validator's output and presents it so you can search it and see the big picture.

The home page has two sets of tabs. The first, Look up an AS, Check an AS path and Routes seen in BGP, holds the three tools, one at a time; the result appears under the tool. The second, Overview, Recent changes and ASPA quality, holds the data. The tabs work with the arrow keys, Home and End. Every address keeps working: #AS680 opens the lookup, #path=… the path check, #routes=… the routes, and #overview, #changes and #quality open the data tabs. The full list of ASPAs, which used to be a table here, is a download on the API page (an old #all link goes there).

At the top of the page a line summarises the data: the number of ASPAs, the time of the last validation run (always in UTC) and the version of the validator that produced it.

Looking up an AS

Type an AS number in the first tab at the top and press Look up. All of these are accepted: AS680, as 680 and 680. AS numbers go up to 4294967295. Anything else shows a short hint instead of a result.

The result has a header with the AS number and two outside links, bgp.tools and RIPEstat, that open in a new tab. Below it are two columns:

Its own ASPA
The providers that this AS authorizes, shown as clickable chips. For each ASPA object the page also lists the RIR (the trust anchor it comes from), its validity period (not before to not after) and the repository address of the signed object. If the AS has not published an ASPA the page says so: its upstream relationships cannot be verified.
Named as a provider
Every ASPA, from any customer, that lists this AS as a provider, with the number of such ASPAs. The list is empty for ASes that nobody names as a provider.

Long lists are cut after a few dozen chips; use +N more to expand them. Click any chip to jump to that AS.

Under the history and the quality, Neighbourhood draws the AS as a graph: its providers above it, with an arrow from the AS to each, the ASes whose ASPA lists it as a provider below it, and two ASes that list each other as a single red box. The button Add the neighbours that the routes show reads the routes of the AS (as the routes box does) and draws on the right the six most seen neighbours that its ASPA does not list, in orange with a dashed outline when the routes make them look like providers. Every box is a link that looks that AS up. The graph is a picture of what the lookup already says in words.

An AS0 · no transit providers chip is not a link. It is the way an AS declares that it has no transit providers, as networks at the top of the hierarchy do. In the wording of the IETF drafts it is an AS0 ASPA: a statement by the registering AS that it has no transit providers (draft-ietf-sidrops-aspa-verification (opens in a new tab), section 4), which the ASPA profile (opens in a new tab) allows only as the single element of the list (section 3.3). It does not say that the AS has no peers: it says that it buys transit from nobody.

Recent checks. When you come back to an AS that you looked up before, a line at the top says what changed since then (providers added or dropped, an ASPA that appeared or went away, ASPAs that started or stopped naming it), and the list Recent checks under the tools shows the last twenty ASes and paths that you checked, each with what is different now. The list is kept in the local storage of your browser and nowhere else: nothing is sent to the site or to anybody, nobody else sees it, and Clear the list deletes it. If your browser refuses local storage, the list is simply not there.

The overview

The tiles

ASPAs
Customer ASes that have a valid ASPA.
Provider ASes
Distinct ASes that appear as a provider in at least one ASPA. AS0 is not counted.
Median providers
The middle value of the number of providers per ASPA, with the size of the largest provider set underneath.
AS0 ASPAs
ASPAs whose only provider is AS0: the customer declares that it has no transit providers.
Coverage
The share of the networks seen in BGP that have an ASPA, from the daily adoption numbers.

The charts, on the statistics page

The charts are on the statistics page, which the overview points to.

ASPAs over time
How the ASPAs evolved day by day since 12 October 2023, the day ASPA objects started to be validated. Show chooses what is drawn: the ASPAs stacked by RIR (AFRINIC has published none so far), their total, the provider ASes, the median number of providers per ASPA, the no-provider ASPAs (AS0), or the coverage, the share of the ASes seen by the RIPE RIS route collectors that have an ASPA (one sample a week before 16 October 2026). Period narrows the chart to the last 12 months or 90 days, and the logarithmic scale, available for the single-line charts, makes a small start and a steep rise readable together. Hover over the chart, or focus it and use the left and right arrow keys (Page Up, Page Down, Home and End go further), to read a single day; Show as a table lists the last day of each month, and the daily data can be downloaded as CSV or JSON. The days up to 15 October 2026 are computed from the daily output of the RIPE NCC RPKI repository archive (opens in a new tab); from then on a daily snapshot of the validator behind this page is added. A few days with a partial or missing archive are left out, so the line may cross them in a straight stretch.
ASPAs by RIR
How many ASPAs each trust anchor (AFRINIC, APNIC, ARIN, LACNIC, RIPE NCC) validates. Each ASPA is counted once, under the trust anchor of its first source object. The percentage in the tooltip is relative to all ASPAs.
Providers per ASPA
A column chart of how many providers each customer declares, from 1 to 9 and then 10+. An ASPA with AS0 counts as one provider.
Most-named providers
The ten ASes that appear in the most ASPAs as a provider. Select a bar to look that AS up.

Hover over a bar or column, or move the keyboard focus onto it, to see the exact value in a tooltip.

Checking an AS path

The Check an AS path box applies the ASPA verification procedure to a path and shows why it passes or fails. It runs in your browser, on the ASPA objects listed on the page: nothing you type is sent anywhere.

Paste the path as your router shows it, with the neighbour first and the origin last, for example 3356 1299 680 (a leading AS is optional). Then say where the route comes from:

A provider (downstream check)
The route was received from a provider. A valid path climbs from the origin through customer-to-provider hops, may have one lateral hop at the top, and then descends. The verdict combines an up-ramp (counted from the origin) and a down-ramp (counted from the neighbour).
A customer or a peer (upstream check)
The route was received from a customer or a peer. Every hop, from the origin to the neighbour, must be a customer-to-provider hop.

For each pair of adjacent ASes the table shows whether the customer's ASPA lists the other AS as a provider: lists (✓), does not list (✗), or has no ASPA (?). An AS whose ASPA contains only AS0 declares that it has no providers.

Valid
The attested hops are enough to show that the path has the expected shape.
Unknown
No hop is attested as wrong, but some ASes publish no ASPA, so the path cannot be proven valid.
Invalid
At least one hop is attested as not customer-to-provider where the procedure needs it to be: the path contains a valley, which is how a route leak or a forged path segment looks.

Limits: repeated ASes (prepending) are removed before checking; paths with an AS_SET are not accepted (the procedure treats them as Invalid); the neighbour check and route-origin validation are not part of this tool; and the result reflects the validated ASPA snapshot shown on this page, not your own router's view. The procedure follows the IETF draft BGP AS_PATH Verification Based on ASPA Objects (opens in a new tab), which is still work in progress.

Background. The downstream procedure is analysed in A. Prado, M. Centenaro and L. Vangelista, Securing Multi-domain 5G/6G Routing: Towards Path Verification with ASPA, IEEE Conference on Standards for Communications and Networking (CSCN), London, 7–9 September 2026 (proceedings forthcoming in IEEE Xplore). The paper shows that downstream ASPA validity is equivalent to a unimodality test on the compressed AS path.

Download the paper (PDF, 7 pages) (opens in a new tab). This is the author's accepted version, posted for personal use. This work has been accepted for publication in the proceedings of IEEE CSCN 2026; copyright may be transferred without notice, after which this version may no longer be accessible. © 2026 IEEE.

Many paths at once. Under the form, Check many paths at once takes one path on each line (for example the paths of a show bgp printed by a script; a prefix in front of a path is kept in the table; empty lines and lines starting with # are skipped, up to 5000 lines) and gives the verdict and a few words of reason for each, with the totals. The table can be downloaded as CSV or JSON; nothing is sent anywhere for that, it is made in your browser.

Checking the routes seen in BGP

The Check the routes seen in BGP box takes a prefix (193.0.0.0/21), an address (193.0.0.1, alone or as a /32 or /128: the routes of the most specific prefix that covers it) or an AS number (AS3333), fetches the routes that the RIPE RIS (opens in a new tab) route collectors see right now, and applies the ASPA verification to every distinct AS path. For an AS the routes are the ones it originates.

The result is a count of valid, unknown and invalid routes and a table of the distinct paths, invalid first, with the reason (the hop that is attested as wrong, or the ASes that publish no ASPA) and a details button that opens the hop-by-hop check of that path right under the row, with a link to open it in the AS path check. The downstream procedure is used, because most of the networks that feed a route collector send it their full table, as a provider does to a customer, and that includes routes they learned from their own providers (the upstream procedure would wrongly call those leaks). A path is invalid when it contains a valley: the climb from the origin and the descent from the neighbour do not meet. Paths with an AS_SET are left out. An AS that originates thousands of prefixes can have an answer too large to load: check one of its prefixes instead.

Above the paths, Origin validation (ROAs) checks every distinct prefix and origin of the routes against the ROAs that the validator has validated, the way a router does route origin validation: valid (a ROA matches the origin and the length), not found (no ROA covers the prefix) or invalid (a ROA covers it but for another AS, or the prefix is longer than the ROA allows). The invalid ones are listed with the reason. At most 200 prefix and origin pairs are checked each time; the line above the tiles says when there are more. This is the other half of the picture: the ROA tells whether the origin is authorized for the prefix, the ASPA whether the path to it makes sense.

Under the paths, the neighbours of the origin table has an Evidence column that says which of them look like providers of the origin, judged from the routes themselves. A neighbour counts as a provider when the route of the origin passes through it up to a Tier-1 network (the clique of the CAIDA relationships, which changes rarely): a peer, or a provider that is not asked to carry the route upwards, passes upwards only the routes that it learned from its customers, so a route that reaches a Tier-1 through a neighbour means that the origin is that neighbour's customer. A Tier-1 next to the origin is not a provider by position alone (Tier-1 networks peer with some others): it needs another Tier-1 on its left. Paths of two ASes say nothing, because the left AS is only the network that feeds the collector, and exchange-point ASes are left out of the paths. Provider means seen by at least three feeders and on at least a fifth of the informative paths; weak means seen, but by fewer feeders or on a smaller share (a route leak can fake a little of it). No evidence is not proof of a peer: the collectors see only part of the Internet. When the ASPA of the origin does not list a neighbour with strong evidence, the page says so: the routes through it do not pass where the ASPA is checked. The routes are the last table dump of the collectors, which can be up to eight hours old; this is a reading of today's routes, not of a monthly dataset.

When the ASPAs prove a path wrong, a Route leaks (valleys) seen now table groups the paths by the place of the valley: the two ASes it lies between, the ASes inside it (the ones to look at first), and how many paths, routes and feeders see it, with a link that opens the path in the path check. A valley is a route that came down from a provider or a peer and went up again: a route leak, or a forged path. The ASPAs prove it only where they are published, so paths of unknown verdict may hide others. And the valley can be the fault of an ASPA, not of a leaker: an ASPA that does not list one of its real providers makes valid routes look like valleys, so read the table together with the evidence about the providers of the origin, just below it.

For an AS, A starting point for the ASPA follows the neighbours table: the providers with strong evidence are proposed (the ones the ASPA already lists stay, whether the routes show them or not, because a backup provider is invisible), the neighbours with weak evidence are only a lead to check by hand, and a field with the list is ready to copy. It is a proposal from what the collectors see, not a verdict: only the holder of the AS knows its upstreams. Check it with the checker of the examples page and publish it as that page explains.

Under the paths, Tromboning measures it. In the literature (Edmundson, Ensafi, Feamster and Rexford at Princeton, after Obar and Clement) a route trombones when it starts and ends in the same country but goes far away on the way, which they measure with traceroutes; it is a matter of geography and delay, and it has nothing to do with valleys: a perfectly valid path can trombone. BGP cannot tell it, and the country where an AS is registered is not where it works, so the page does not guess: press Measure it now, a traceroute from up to twelve probes in the country, through Globalping (opens in a new tab), towards an address of one of the prefixes of the AS; each hop is shown with the network it belongs to and its delay, and the page gives the median round trip of the probes together, and for each probe which networks the packet crosses, where the delay rises and stays up, and how long the round trip is from the first public address to the end: normal inside a country up to about 50 ms, longer than it should be above that, and a trombone at 100 ms or more (the delay of a packet that crosses an ocean), supposing that the target is in the country of the probes. A delay is not a distance (queues, tunnels, the access network of the probe and slow answers add milliseconds), so it is a lead, not proof. Nothing starts until you press the button; Globalping and RIPEstat see your address and what is asked, and the test counts against the hourly limit of your address. The probes start in the country where the AS is registered (from the delegation files of the RIRs), which is an approximation and sometimes a bold one: an AS can work elsewhere, or in several countries, and the address that is measured may not be in that country; then a long delay is the distance, not a trombone. A menu next to the button lets you choose another country for the probes.

IPv4 and IPv6, on the same probes. When the AS has a prefix of each family in the routes, a box under the button, Compare IPv4 and IPv6 on the same probes, runs the traceroute over IPv6 first (which picks probes that can use it) and then over IPv4 on the very same probes (Globalping reuses the places of the first measurement for the second). The page gives the median round trip of each family and their difference, how many probes are faster over each, how many answer on one family only, and for each probe the networks that the packet crosses over IPv4 and over IPv6, so that a route that trombones only in one family, or goes through different networks, stands out. The two addresses are different addresses of the same AS, not the same host, and a difference of a few milliseconds is noise. It uses twice the measurements of a single run, and the result can be shared like the others.

Where the delays come from. There is no single threshold in the literature. The LACNIC 2022 Connectivity Study (opens in a new tab) (Formoso and Cicileo, 2023) finds that the round trips that stay inside a country are mostly below 50 ms, uses 20 ms to tell two nearby addresses apart, and notes that in Brazil and Argentina the measurements that leave the country are typically above 100 ms. Shah, Fontugne and Papadopoulos (opens in a new tab) confirm detours seen in BGP with traceroutes, by the countries of the hops and by a rise of the delay of ten times or more between two hops (summary on RIPE Labs (opens in a new tab)); the detours between close countries, such as Italy and France, were missed by the delay. Edmundson and others (opens in a new tab) measured the detours of paths through surveillance states with traceroutes and geolocation. The bands used here (up to 50 ms, up to 100 ms, 100 ms and more) follow those figures; they are a reading, not a standard.

For an AS, under the paths, Watch the updates live connects your browser to RIPE RIS Live (opens in a new tab), which hands out the BGP updates that the route collectors receive as they arrive (not the table dump of the other results, which can be eight hours old), and checks every announcement with the ASPAs: time, feeder, prefix, path and verdict, the last fifteen, with the totals. Choose the updates announced by the AS (few, for a small network) or going through it (many, for a transit network). It stops after five minutes or 300 announcements, or when you press Stop. A route leak is a thing that happens in an update: this is where one would show up while it is happening.

Privacy. Unlike the AS path check, this box does send what you type, the prefix or the AS number, to RIPEstat (opens in a new tab) in your browser, and receives the routes from there; the page's own server does not see it. The live watcher connects from your browser to RIPE (RIS Live), which sees your address as any site you visit does; nothing goes through this site. The ROA checks, instead, are asked of this site (which asks the validator), one request per prefix and origin; they are not sent to anyone else.

What if the ASPA listed other providers?

For an AS, under the proposal of the ASPA, What if the ASPA of ASn listed… repeats the check of the routes above as if the ASPA of that AS had the providers you type (the proposal is already in the box; AS0 alone means no provider). Every other ASPA stays as published. It shows the routes that would be valid, unknown and invalid, before and after, and the paths whose verdict changes, most routes first. A route through a provider that you leave out becomes Invalid for the networks that check ASPAs, so it is the test to run before publishing. It checks the routes the collectors see now, from the same view as the table; it does not know the routes that only exist for customers or peers that the collectors do not see.

Recent changes and the history of an AS

Recent changes lists, for each of the last 14 days, the ASPAs that appeared (new), changed their providers (the providers added are marked +, the ones dropped −) or disappeared. The same list is available as an Atom feed. Looking up an AS adds a History block with every change of its own ASPA and the day an ASPA first named it as a provider.

The changes since 12 October 2023 were reconstructed from the daily output of the RIPE NCC RPKI repository archive (opens in a new tab); since 16 October 2026 they come from the validator behind this page, which can show a brief disappearance when a repository was unreachable. An ASPA that already existed on 12 October 2023 is shown as new on that day.

On the page of an AS, Compare two dates shows what changed for it between two days: the providers its ASPA added or dropped, the ASPAs that began or stopped naming it as a provider and, for the days that have a file, the places of valley that involve it (the daily files of the last 60 days are kept). The state is the one at the end of each day.

ASPA quality

The ASPA quality section runs checks on every ASPA listed on the page and shows how many ASPAs have an error (something that cannot be right), a warning (very likely worth fixing) or a note (worth a look). Each group opens into the list of ASPAs, with a short explanation of why it matters. The checks are:

AS0 together with other providers (error)
AS0 means no transit provider; listed next to real providers the ASPA contradicts itself, and the profile forbids it.
An AS listed as its own provider, or a private or reserved provider AS number (error)
Such an entry has no meaning: no real path goes through a private, documentation or reserved AS number.
Two ASes that list each other as provider (warning)
A customer-to-provider link has a direction. When both ASes list each other (siblings may do it on purpose) a path can climb and descend over that link, which weakens the detection of route leaks.
A provider not seen in BGP (warning)
The RIPE RIS collectors see no route through that AS: a typo, a retired AS, or an AS that announces nothing.
An ASPA that expires within 30 days (warning)
When the signed object expires the ASPA disappears and the AS's routes go back to unknown until a new one is published.
A customer not seen in BGP, or a provider set of 30 or more ASes (notes)
An AS the collectors do not see may announce only where they do not look; with a very large provider set almost any path through the AS is accepted.

The checks that need to know which ASes appear in BGP use a list refreshed every day (visibility.json, from the RIPEstat call ris-asns) and are left out when it cannot be loaded. A finding is a reason to look, not a verdict: a mutual pair may be intentional, and an AS can be absent from the collectors for good reasons. In the lookup, the Quality block lists the findings of that AS's own ASPA.

In Check the routes seen in BGP, a table of the neighbours of the origin lists the AS right before the origin in each path, how many routes go through it, whether the origin's ASPA lists it, and how many of those routes are valid, unknown or invalid. It also shows the providers that are listed but not seen. A neighbour that is a peer or a customer is not meant to be in an ASPA, so not listed is a gap only for a neighbour that is a provider; the invalid routes through not-listed neighbours are the ones to look at first.

Statistics

The statistics page holds the numbers behind the ASPAs. First the charts of the ASPAs that this validator considers valid: ASPAs over time (daily since October 2023; stacked by RIR, or the total, the provider ASes, the median providers per ASPA, the no-provider ASPAs or the coverage, over any period, with a logarithmic scale and a download of the daily data as CSV or JSON), ASPAs by RIR, providers per ASPA and the most-named providers, each of which opens its lookup.

Networks with an ASPA over time

A chart on the statistics page of how many networks have an ASPA, month by month since the first one, for all of them, for each RIR or for a country (where the AS number was registered; an ASPA of a network that is not seen in BGP counts as unknown). The numbers are replayed from the events of the history and rebuilt once a day (/api/v1/trends.json). Under the chart is the HTML to show the same chart on another page: /embed.html?country=IT or /embed.html?rir=ripe in an iframe. The ASPAs over time chart has a line for every RIR, AFRINIC included, which runs along zero until it publishes its first ASPA.

Adoption

Then who has an ASPA and who has not. The base is the set of networks (ASes) that the RIPE RIS collectors see in BGP; coverage is the share of them with a valid ASPA. It is shown:

  • by size of the network, from the customer cone: the networks that one reaches through its customers, counting those of its customers' customers and so on, taken from the CAIDA AS Relationships dataset (opens in a new tab). A network with no customers is a stub. The cone is inferred from observed routes, so it is an estimate and tends to be generous for large networks;
  • by RIR, the registry where the AS number was registered (from the delegation files of the five RIRs), which is not always the trust anchor that holds the ASPA;
  • by country, the country recorded in those files: where the number was registered, not necessarily where the network operates. Only countries with a minimum number of networks are listed by default, because a percentage of a handful of networks says little;
  • the 100 largest networks by cone, each with its ASPA state, and a switch to show only those without one.

Route leaks today

Under the title of the page, two small lines give the numbers of the project: how many networks have an ASPA and how many changes to ASPAs have been recorded since 12 October 2023; and, for the routes of today, how many were checked and from how many collectors and feeder networks, the shares of valid, unknown and invalid ones, the places of valley and the routes checked in all the days of the history. A feeder is a network that sends its routes to a collector.

Below the adoption, the statistics page checks every route of 30 collectors (sixteen of RIPE RIS, rrc00, rrc01, rrc03, rrc04, rrc05, rrc06, rrc07, rrc10, rrc11, rrc12, rrc14, rrc15, rrc16, rrc19, rrc21 and rrc23, and fourteen of RouteViews, the other big collection project, with other peers: Oregon, route-views3 and 4, London, Palo Alto, Tokyo, New York, Perth, Atlanta, Singapore, Johannesburg, Sao Paulo, Sydney and Equinix; hundreds of millions of routes in their table dumps) with the downstream ASPA verification, once a day, and groups the ones that the ASPAs prove to contain a valley by the place of the valley. A valley is a route that came down from a provider or a peer and went up again. Two tables tell the places apart by what the CAIDA relationships say of the first AS after the climb, the AS right after the part of the path that the ASPAs show to go up from the origin:

  • Possible route leaks: that AS is a peer or a customer of the AS before it, and it passed the route on up. It is the AS in the valley to look at.
  • ASPAs that seem to miss a provider: that AS is a provider of the AS before it, which has an ASPA that does not list it: valid routes through it look like valleys. The owner of that ASPA is the one to tell. Most of the places are of this kind.

Places with no relation in the dataset are counted but not listed. The relations are inferred from observed routes, so they can be out of date, and a place is a candidate, not a verdict. The ASPAs prove a valley only where they are published: a large share of the routes cross an AS without an ASPA and stay unknown. A leak seen by one feeder only can be a leak at the feeder itself. A feeder is a network that sends its routes to a collector; one that feeds several collectors counts once. The same file is available as JSON.

Over time is the chart of the places of possible leak and of gap, one point a day (/api/v1/leaks-history.json). It is one point a day since 17 October 2026; the days before were rebuilt afterwards from the table dumps of 00:00 UTC of each day, with the ASPAs as they were that day (replayed from the history of changes) and the relationships dataset of today, and a day that could not be rebuilt is missing. A change can come from the routes, from the ASPAs published that day or from the relationships dataset.

Collectors compared has one bar for each of the 30 collectors, coloured by project (RIPE RIS or RouteViews), for the figure chosen in the menu: the places of valley it sees, the places that only that collector sees, its invalid routes as a share of the routes it reads, its feeder networks, or the routes it reads. A collector that sees many places alone has peers that the others do not have. RIS and RouteViews on the same places tells the same places apart by who sees them (only RIS, both, only RouteViews), for all the places, for the possible leaks, for the gaps in ASPAs and for the places seen by three feeders or more. A place that only one project sees is not wrong: it can come from a peer that the other does not have, or from a leak that lasts a few hours, because the tables are taken at different times.

From allocation to ASPA

For each RIR, region and economy: how many AS numbers are allocated (from the delegation files of the five RIRs), how many of those are advertised in BGP (seen by the RIPE RIS collectors) and how many have an ASPA, with the share each step keeps, and the reserved and available numbers of each registry. For the economies and regions the table also gives the ASPAs and the advertised networks per million Internet users, so that a large country does not hide a small one; population, Internet users and the regions come from the World Bank open data (the most recent value of each economy). By age of the AS number gives the share of the advertised numbers that have an ASPA by the year of the allocation and for 16-bit and 32-bit numbers. The economy is where the number was registered, not where it is used. The file is /api/v1/resources.json, rebuilt once a day.

ASPAs that name AS numbers that should not be there lists the customers and the providers of ASPAs that no registry has allocated (they are listed as available or reserved, are private, reserved or for documentation, or are in no delegation file): a mistake, or a test that stayed in the RPKI.

Valid routes by address space and IPv6 against IPv4

The routes of the 30 collectors again, with two other ways of counting them. Valid routes by address space weighs each route by the addresses it covers (IPv6 in /48s) instead of counting it once, and tells the verdicts apart by the number of ASes in the path; the same space seen by several collectors counts each time. IPv6 and IPv4 side by side puts the two families in one table with the ratio IPv6 / IPv4: routes, origin ASes, shares of valid, unknown and invalid routes, the valid share of the address space, and the average length of the AS path, also weighted by address space. Most routes are unknown because most networks have no ASPA yet; only a route whose every hop is covered by an ASPA is valid.

Invalid announcements, as they happen

Every ten minutes the site listens for about nine minutes to the stream of BGP updates of all the RIPE RIS collectors (RIS Live), checks every announcement with the downstream verification and keeps the counts by hour, origin AS and prefix for 14 days. The section shows the share of invalid announcements by hour, the profile (announcements per second, peaks) and the origins with the most invalid announcements, the ones with the highest share (at least 100 announcements) and the prefixes with the most, in the manner of an instability report. It is a sample of the stream, from the collectors and the feeders that RIPE has; a bad path that many feeders repeat shows up many times. The report is /api/v1/invalid.json.

Validators compared

Every hour the site compares the ASPAs of three validators: the Routinator of this site (NLnet Labs), rpki-client (opens in a new tab) (OpenBSD, the console of the project) and the rpki-client that Cloudflare runs. They read the same repositories with different code, so they are an independent check on each other. The section shows how many ASPAs each has and when it was built, how many customers have the same providers in all three, and the ASPAs on which they differ with a possible reason. A few differences are normal (they read at different moments, and each can reject an object that another accepts); one that lasts is worth a report to the validator. The common one is an ASPA that lists AS0 next to other providers, which the profile forbids: Routinator keeps it and rpki-client rejects or trims it. The same data is available as JSON.

The adoption numbers are rebuilt once a day and available as JSON. ASPAs of networks that the collectors do not see are not counted. The page used to be called Adoption.

Writing your ASPA

The examples page explains what to put in an ASPA (list every provider, backups included; list providers only, never peers, customers or an exchange point) with five cases, the common mistakes and a checker: type your AS number and the providers you intend to list, and it points out contradictions (AS0 with other providers, an AS as its own provider, private numbers) and compares your list with the ASPA that is published now. Nothing is sent anywhere except a request to this site for the published ASPA.

Status

The status page (the last item of the menu at the top) shows when each part was last updated: the validator, the per-AS files of the API, the daily history, the BGP visibility file used by the quality checks, the adoption numbers, the route leaks and the notification service. A part is late when it is older than expected and down when it is much older or does not answer. The page checks from your browser, so it shows what you would get, and refreshes every minute. The same checks give the dot next to Status in the menu of every page: green when all the checks are green, red when all are red, orange otherwise (a late check counts as not green). The result is kept for a minute, so moving between pages does not repeat the checks.

Under the table, Over time shows the state of each part in the last 24 hours, 7, 30 or 90 days, one strip for each part: a server-side job runs the same checks every 15 minutes and keeps the result, and each cell of a strip shows the worst state in its stretch of time (green OK, orange late, red down, grey no sample, for instance while the job did not run). Next to each strip is the share of the checks that found the part OK; hover a strip, or focus it and use the left and right arrows, to read a cell. Below are the last problems with their start and length, and a table with the same figures. The history is also a file, /api/v1/status-history.json: a list of checks and, for each moment, one letter per check (o OK, l late, d down).

Availability by month sums it up: for each month the share of the checks that found each part OK, kept for good (the chart above keeps 90 days), with the number of checks recorded and what share of a full month that is, so that a month with gaps is not mistaken for a perfect one. The numbers are in /api/v1/status-months.json.

Sharing, badges, feeds and the API

Every AS has a page of its own, https://aspa.ping6.it/as/AS8283: it is this same page, already showing that AS, so it can be bookmarked or linked. Under each result, Share and follow has the links to that page, to an Atom feed of its changes (its own ASPA, and the ASPAs of others that add or drop it as a provider, so a provider can follow who starts to name it) and to the JSON of the AS, and Add the badge to your site gives a small image, with Markdown and HTML to paste, that says how many providers the AS has authorized.

The API page describes the JSON, the badge and the feed, and has the download of the whole list of ASPAs as CSV or JSON, for one RIR or all, and there is an OpenAPI description. The files are rebuilt every 15 minutes from the same data as this page, so a change can take up to half an hour to appear there.

To be told instead of having to look, see notifications below.

Notifications by e-mail and webhook

Under each result, Get notified by e-mail or webhook subscribes you to one AS. You receive a message when its own ASPA appears, changes or disappears, and when another ASPA adds or drops it as a provider (the same events as its feed). The validator is checked every ten minutes, and a change is announced only when two checks in a row show it, so that a repository that is unreachable for a moment does not cause a false alarm.

A box under the form asks, as an extra, for the places of valley that involve the AS: once a day, when a new one appears (the AS inside a valley, or its ASPA that seems to miss a provider), you get one message each. The places that exist when you subscribe are not announced, only the new ones. The box is chosen when you subscribe; to change it, stop and subscribe again. A webhook receives these as aspa.leak (see the API page).

A second extra, Also tell me when AS… is the origin of N invalid announcements in ten minutes, watches the routes as they happen: every ten minutes the server listens to the stream of RIPE RIS Live, from all its collectors, for about nine minutes, checks each announcement with the ASPAs (the same downstream procedure as the rest of the site) and counts, for each origin AS, the ones that are invalid. When the AS of your subscription reaches the threshold you chose (5, 10, 50, 100, 500 or 1000), you get one message with the count, its share and the prefixes with most, and then no other for six hours, however long it lasts. The first message can come ten minutes after the start of the problem, never sooner. A webhook receives it as aspa.invalid. Like the places of valley, it is a lead: an invalid announcement is a valley in the path, which is a leak, a forged path, or an ASPA that misses a provider.

The Status page has the same subscription for the state of the service: a message when a part (the validator, the API, the daily jobs) becomes late or down, and when it is back to OK. As for the ASPAs, a change is announced when two checks in a row, 15 minutes apart, show it, and the parts that changed in the same check come in one message. The monitor is a part as well: if no check has been recorded for three quarters of an hour, you are told that nothing is being watched. A webhook receives these as aspa.status. If the server itself is down no message can be sent, so this is not a replacement for a monitor of your own.

E-mail

Type your address. A message with a link comes to it; nothing is sent until you open the link and press the button. Every notification has a link to stop, and a List-Unsubscribe header that mail programs show as an unsubscribe button. The messages come from notifications@aspa.ping6.it, are signed with DKIM and are plain text. If they land in the spam folder, mark them as not spam.

Webhook

Give an https address with a public host name (port 443 or 8443; not an IP address, not a private network). The service first sends a verification request with a random challenge, and the address must answer with a 2xx status and repeat the challenge in the body; only then the subscription is active, and the secret is shown once. Each delivery is a JSON POST signed in the X-ASPA-Signature header (HMAC-SHA256 of the timestamp, a dot and the body, with the secret). The API page has the format. A webhook that fails five deliveries in a row is switched off.

What is kept

The address (or the webhook address and its secret), the AS (or the status alerts) and the dates of subscription and of the last notification, for as long as the subscription lasts. A request that is never confirmed is deleted after two days. Visitor addresses are not stored: they are only counted in memory to limit abuse, a few requests an hour. Nothing is used for anything else or given to anyone; stopping deletes the record. Questions: antonio.prado@dei.unipd.it.

This is a research service run without guarantees: a notification may be late or, if a mail server rejects the message, lost.

Sharing a result

Share this result, under the result of a path check or of many paths, turns it into a link such as https://aspa.ping6.it/r/0123456789ab that you can send to a colleague or cite. The page of the link shows the result as it was, with the time and the data it was computed with, and a link to run the same check again with today's data. A chat or a social network shows the title and a short summary when the link is pasted.

What is kept is exactly what the page sends when you press the button: a title, a few sentences and a table, written by the page and checked by the service (it takes only plain text, up to 500 table rows and 200 KB). It is kept for 30 days and then deleted. Anybody who has the link can read it, so do not share a result you would not show. The address of the visitor is not stored: it is only counted in memory, 20 links an hour, to limit abuse. The link is not signed: it shows what the page computed, not a statement by anybody. The report is also available as JSON at /api/v1/reports/<id>.

Links and keyboard use

Every AS has a page of its own, https://aspa.ping6.it/as/AS680, and the routes of a prefix or an AS have an address too, https://aspa.ping6.it/#routes=AS3333.

An AS-path check has its own address too, such as https://aspa.ping6.it/#path=3356+680&from=up (from=up for a route received from a customer or a peer, from=down for one received from a provider).

Each lookup has its own address: https://aspa.ping6.it/#AS680 opens the page already showing AS680, so you can bookmark or share a result. The same works for any AS number.

Everything can be used from the keyboard: the lookup field, the tabs (with the arrow keys, Home and End), chips, chart bars and the buttons are all reachable with Tab, and the tooltips follow the focus. The page follows your system's light or dark setting and adapts to narrow screens.

A quick look at any AS. A click on the name of an AS, in a table, a list of providers or a path, opens a small window with what the site knows about it: whether it publishes an ASPA and with which providers, how many ASPAs name it as a provider, whether the route collectors see it, the findings on its ASPA and its last change, with a link to its page. The names in the window open the next one. Escape closes it; a click with Ctrl, Cmd or Shift goes straight to the page of the AS, as before. The window asks this site for one small file (/api/v1/as/<number>) and nothing else.

Where the data comes from

The data comes from Routinator (opens in a new tab), the RPKI validator run at rpki.ping6.it (opens in a new tab). It validates the RPKI repositories of all five RIRs and the page reads three of its outputs:

  • the list of valid ASPAs (jsonext (opens in a new tab), route origins and router keys left out), and
  • the validator status, which supplies the last validation time and the Routinator version, and
  • the origin validation of a route (prefix and origin), when you check the routes seen in BGP.

The chart of ASPAs over time, the changes and the quality checks read four more files, history.json, events.json, changes.atom and visibility.json, from the history branch of the public project (opens in a new tab), where a daily job adds one row and the day's changes. The routes seen in BGP come from RIPEstat, in your browser, when you ask for them.

The numbers behind the statistics page and the country of each AS are built once a day from public datasets: the delegation files of the five RIRs (the country and the registry of each AS number), the CAIDA AS Relationships dataset (opens in a new tab) (the size of a network, the Tier-1 list and the exchange-point ASes) and the list of the ASes that the RIPE RIS collectors see. The JSON, the badge and the feed of each AS are built every 15 minutes from the validator's list and the history files.

The validator refreshes about every 10 minutes and the web server keeps its answer for 2 minutes, so a new ASPA can take a little while to show up here. The last validation time in the header tells you how fresh the data is.

Reading the results

  • No ASPA is not an error. Many ASes have not published one yet. It only means the page has nothing to show for them.
  • The page shows what this validator sees. Another validator, or the same one a few minutes later, may differ slightly.
  • An ASPA lists providers for one customer. It says nothing about peers, and it does not check any specific BGP route: that is done by routers that use these objects.
  • Validity dates are those in the signed object. An expired ASPA is not shown, because the validator discards it.
  • A valley is not always a leak. An ASPA that does not list one of its real providers makes valid routes look like valleys; read the table of leaks together with the evidence about the providers.
  • Evidence and tromboning candidates are readings, not verdicts. They come from the routes that the collectors see now (a table dump up to eight hours old). No evidence is not proof of a peer, and the country where an AS is registered is not where it works.

If something does not load

If the page shows Could not load the ASPA data, the validator is probably restarting. Wait a minute and reload. If it persists, the error text in the message shows which request failed. If a number or a table looks old, open the status page (the last item of the menu, with a dot): it says which piece is late.

Accessibility

ASPA Explorer is built to be usable by everyone, including people who use a keyboard, a screen reader, magnification or high-contrast settings. The target is conformance with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 (opens in a new tab) at level AA. The pages have been checked with the axe-core automated testing tool in both the light and the dark theme, with no issues reported. They have not been audited by an independent body or tested with screen-reader users, so there may be gaps that an automated tool cannot find.

What is in place

  • Keyboard. Every control can be reached and used with Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter and Space. The focused element has a visible outline. A Skip to main content link appears first when you press Tab.
  • Structure. Pages use landmarks (header, main, footer) and a single heading hierarchy. The table has a caption and column headers, and every form field has a label.
  • Charts. Each bar or column, and the chart of ASPAs over time, can take keyboard focus, carries its label and value as text, and shows its tooltip on focus as well as on hover. The exact numbers are also in the table (for the time chart, a table of month ends) and in the lookup results, so no information depends on seeing the chart.
  • Colour. Text and controls meet the WCAG AA contrast ratio in the light and the dark theme, which follow your system setting. Colour is never the only way something is shown.
  • Screen readers. Lookup and path-check results are announced when they appear. Links in the footer open in a new tab and say so in their accessible name. Decorative images are hidden from assistive technology and meaningful ones have a text alternative.
  • Zoom and small screens. The layout reflows without horizontal scrolling down to a width of 320 pixels, which is what a desktop window shows at 400% zoom.
  • Motion. If your system asks for reduced motion, the page does not animate scrolling.

Known limitations

  • The tables and chips can be long: a large provider set or the long table are tiring to read with a screen reader. Use the lookup or the AS filter to narrow them down.
  • The outside links (bgp.tools, RIPEstat) and the validator's raw JSON are not under this project's control.

About this page

ASPA Explorer is a static page with no external dependencies and no cookies, with a small service behind it. Your lookups and path checks happen in your browser and are not sent anywhere; the routes box sends what you type to RIPEstat and asks this site, which asks the validator, about the ROAs, as said above; and a subscription to notifications keeps an address, as described in its section. Apart from that, the only thing this site learns about you is what any web server learns from a request; the footer shows the address your request came from, as the server sees it, and links it to RIPEstat; nothing is sent to RIPEstat unless you follow the link.
