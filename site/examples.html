Writing your ASPA

What to put in the ASPA of your network, with the usual cases, the usual mistakes and a checker for your own list.

The rules in short

An ASPA is a signed statement by the holder of an AS number, the customer, naming the ASes that are allowed to be its providers: the networks it buys transit from, to which it sends its routes and the routes of its own customers so that they are announced to the world. Routers that check ASPAs use it to refuse a route that went from a customer up to a provider that the customer never named.

  • List every provider you have, the backup ones too, and a DDoS scrubbing centre that announces your routes (see the case below). A route that reaches the world through an unlisted provider is judged invalid by those who check.
  • List providers only. Not your peers, not your customers, not an exchange point or its route server, with one exception: a route server that puts its own AS number in the paths counts as a provider (see the case below). The ASPA says who sits above you in the paths, nothing else.
  • One ASPA per AS number. When you add or drop a provider, change it. Add the new provider to the ASPA before you start sending routes through it.
  • A network that buys transit from nobody publishes AS0 alone (an AS0 ASPA: it has no transit providers; it can still have peers). Never AS0 together with other providers.
  • The examples use AS numbers reserved for documentation (AS64496 to AS64511) and, where they need more networks, for private use (from AS64512); replace them with yours. The checker below calls a private number an error, because a real ASPA must not name one.

Cases

One upstream

AS64500 is a small company that buys Internet access from a single provider, AS64510.

customer AS64500
providers AS64510

Several providers, with a backup

AS64501 is connected to two providers, AS64510 and AS64511, and has a third one, AS64512, that it uses only if the others fail.

customer AS64501
providers AS64510, AS64511, AS64512

The backup provider must be there too. Without it, the day the main links fail and the routes go through AS64512, they look like a leak to everybody who checks.

A transit network

AS64510 sells transit to AS64500 and AS64501, and buys it from AS64520 and AS64521. Its ASPA names the networks above it. Its customers are not in it: each customer names AS64510 in its own ASPA.

customer AS64510
providers AS64520, AS64521

A network with no provider

AS64520 is at the top of the hierarchy: it buys transit from nobody and exchanges routes with the other large networks as peers. It says so with an ASPA whose only provider is AS0, which lets everybody reject any route in which this AS appears as if it had a provider above it. AS0 also states that the network is not a client of a route server that puts its AS number in the paths (see the next case).

customer AS64520
providers AS0

A member of an exchange point, and its route server

AS64501 also exchanges routes at an Internet exchange point (IXP). Instead of a BGP session with each of the other members, it keeps one session with the route server of the exchange, AS64600, which passes the routes of every member on to every other member; the traffic itself goes directly between the members over the switching fabric of the exchange. AS64501 also peers bilaterally with AS64530 over the same fabric. Nobody at the exchange sells transit to AS64501, so by trade none of them is its provider.

What counts for ASPA is what the paths show, and the question is whether the route server puts its own AS number in the paths that it passes on.

A transparent route server does not: AS64530 receives the routes of AS64501 with the path AS64501, as if the two peered directly. This is what the route server specification asks for (RFC 7947, section 2.2.2.1 (opens in a new tab)) and what most exchanges do. The route server is then out of the question: the members check each other's routes as routes from a peer, and the rule that the AS added last to the path must be the neighbour is waived for routes that come through the exchange. The ASPA of AS64501 stays as in the case with the backup provider.

customer AS64501
providers AS64510, AS64511, AS64512      (not AS64600, not AS64530)

A non-transparent route server puts its AS number in the path, usually for the sake of old members whose routers refuse a path that does not begin with the neighbour. Say AS64501 is also a client of such a route server, AS64601, at a second exchange: the members there receive AS64601 AS64501. In the paths, AS64601 now stands above AS64501, where a provider would, and the ASPA verification treats it so: an AS must list the non-transparent route servers it is a client of together with its providers (draft-ietf-sidrops-aspa-verification (opens in a new tab), section 4). A member that does not gets every route it sends through that route server judged invalid by the members that check, with no leak anywhere. So the ASPA of AS64501 becomes:

customer AS64501
providers AS64510, AS64511, AS64512, AS64601      (the route server that is in the paths)

Two consequences of the same rule. A network that buys transit from nobody but is a client of a non-transparent route server cannot publish AS0: it lists the route server instead. And the bilateral peer AS64530 is never listed, whichever kind of route server the exchange runs.

To know which kind yours is, ask the exchange, or look at a route received from the route server: if the path begins with the AS number of the route server, it is non-transparent. The path check on the home page says when an AS in a path is an exchange-point AS. The route server itself buys transit from nobody and publishes AS0, as in the case above: when it is in the paths, it is the ASPAs of its members that the verification looks at, not its own.

A DDoS scrubbing centre

AS64502 buys protection against DDoS attacks from a scrubbing centre, AS64540. During an attack, or all the time in an always-on service, AS64502 sends its prefixes to AS64540 over BGP, and AS64540 announces them to the world, filters the traffic and returns the clean part through a tunnel or a private link. In the paths that the world sees, AS64540 stands above AS64502, where a transit provider would: for a router that checks ASPAs it is a hop from a customer to a provider. It belongs in the ASPA with the other providers, whether it carries the traffic every day or only on the day of the attack.

customer AS64502
providers AS64510, AS64511, AS64540      (the scrubbing centre is one of them)

Add it before the first diversion, as for any new provider: in the middle of an attack there is no time to wait for a new ASPA, and until it is published the diverted routes look like a leak to those who check. Some centers announce your prefixes with their own AS number as the origin instead, and you only authorise them in a ROA. Then the center is the origin and not a provider, and your ASPA does not name it. Ask the center which of the two it does.

Common mistakes

Listing a peer or a customer
It lets routes in that should be refused, and it weakens the check for everybody. The quality checks of this site flag an ASPA in which two networks list each other.
Forgetting a provider
The routes that your customers or you announce through it become invalid for the networks that check; an on-demand scrubbing centre is easy to forget, because nothing goes through it until the attack, and so is a route server that puts its AS number in the paths. The routes seen in BGP, in the check on the home page, show the neighbours next to your AS and whether your ASPA lists them.
AS0 together with real providers
It contradicts itself: AS0 means no transit provider. Use AS0 alone or the real providers alone.
Your own number, or a private or reserved one
An AS cannot be its own provider, and private numbers (64512 to 65534, 4200000000 and up) and documentation numbers never appear in the global routing system.
A typo in a provider's number
It names an AS that is not yours. The quality checks flag a provider that the route collectors never see.
Leaving it as it was after a change of provider
Drop the old provider only when no route goes through it any more.

Check your list

Type your AS number and the providers you intend to list. The check runs in your browser; it also asks this site for the ASPA that your AS publishes now, to compare it. Resting the pointer on an AS number asks RIPEstat for its name.

Publishing it

The ASPA is created by the holder of the AS number in the RPKI service of the regional registry that the number comes from. This site shows which trust anchors have ASPAs in them: RIPE NCC, ARIN, APNIC and LACNIC have, AFRINIC none so far (see ASPAs by RIR on the Statistics page). Look for the RPKI or resource-certification section of your registry's portal: RIPE NCC (opens in a new tab), ARIN (opens in a new tab), APNIC (opens in a new tab), LACNIC (opens in a new tab), AFRINIC (opens in a new tab). Each portal has its own screens and the support changes with time, so follow the registry's own instructions.

Once it is published, it takes a while to reach the validators: the repository of the registry, then the validator, which here refreshes about every 10 minutes. Look up your AS on the home page to see it, and subscribe to its changes from there. The Statistics page shows how many networks like yours have done it.
