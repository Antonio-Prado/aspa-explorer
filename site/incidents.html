Would a filter built from the IRR have stopped them?

Many networks accept a route from a customer or a peer only when the IRR has a route object for that prefix and its origin, and some accept the more specifics of a route object as well. For each prefix hijack (PRH) and each more specific announced by another network (DEG-MA) of the last 30 days, this table looks for the origin in the route objects of the IRR databases of the RIRs, as they were on the day of the incident. Route leaks are not checked: their origin is the legitimate one, so its route object says nothing about the leak.

And with the AS-SET of the network that sent them?

A network usually builds the filter of a customer or of a peer from the AS-SET that this one registers: the route objects of every AS in the set, and their more specifics. Here the network that sent the routes is the origin, for a hijack and a more specific, and the AS that leaked, for a route leak; the filter is the one of the network that received them from it. Its set is the one that its aut-num exports to that network, or else the one it gives in PeeringDB; with neither, only its own route objects count. Only this first filter is looked at, not the ones further on.

If more networks published an ASPA

Today few networks publish an ASPA. This chart asks what would change if more did. Across: the share of the networks that publish an ASPA (the one that CAIDA suggests for them). Up: the share of the incidents that the ASPAs would then prove Invalid. The persistent valleys (RLK-PV in the table) are left out of this chart: they are relationships that CAIDA gets wrong, and complete ASPAs built from CAIDA would prove them Invalid by construction. The two lines differ in who publishes first: the networks with the most customers (by CAIDA), or networks taken in a fixed random order.

    How to read this

    • An incident is a candidate. It is a group of updates that one of our detectors flagged, seen by the peers of one collector: not a verdict on any network (no AS number is published here), and not an incident of the Internet. The types that the detectors do not see are missing from the counts; the table of coverage below says which.
    • Invalid, Unknown, Valid are the verdicts of the downstream ASPA procedure on the AS paths that the incident kept: Invalid when the ASPAs prove the path wrong, Unknown when ASPAs are missing, Valid when the hops attested from the two ends cover the whole path (one lateral hop may sit between them). ASPAs of the time are the ASPAs known when the incident began: the newest state of the history of the ASPAs seen by then, never one seen later. Until 15 October 2026 that history is the daily archive of the RIPE NCC, one state a day taken in the early morning, and since then the job of this site, every hour. When an ASPA of the paths changed between the look before the incident and its end, in a way that changes the verdict, the history cannot say which state held: the incident keeps the verdict of the ASPAs known at its start, and the page says how many there are and between which two numbers the count of the Invalid lies. An ASPA judges the AS path, so a wrong origin, flapping and mass withdrawals, which have little or no path, are not expected to be Invalid.
    • Complete ASPAs is the best case: every network publishes the providers that CAIDA infers for it. The valley detector uses the same relationships, so for those incidents the column agrees by construction and measures nothing about ASPA; the ASPAs of the time and the chart are what tells.
    • The period is yours to choose. Everything and the single years add up the days read so far, each day on its own: the leaking networks (FP and PV in the table) are judged within the day, and complete ASPAs come from the CAIDA file of the month. The last 30 days is one window, read as a whole: there a network is a persistent valley when its leaks begin in 12 different hours of the whole window, so the lines FP, PV, UL and LL differ from the sum of the days. A day is the UTC day its incidents ended. The days before 23 October 2026 come from the archive of RIPE RIS, a month at a time: an incident that was still open at the end of a month may be counted again in the next one.
    • The time frame is the one under the title: the updates that one collector received between those two times, in UTC. An incident is counted when it has ended (after 15 minutes without updates), so the last minutes of each file are added with the next one. The page is built once a day from the incident files of the last 30 days: with little data the numbers describe a short window, not the Internet.
