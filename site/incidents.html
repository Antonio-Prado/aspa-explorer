If more networks published an ASPA

Today few networks publish an ASPA. This chart asks what would change if more did. Across: the share of the networks that publish an ASPA (the one that CAIDA suggests for them). Up: the share of the incidents that the ASPAs would then prove Invalid. The persistent valleys (RLK-PV in the table) are left out of this chart: they are relationships that CAIDA gets wrong, and complete ASPAs built from CAIDA would prove them Invalid by construction. The two lines differ in who publishes first: the networks with the most customers (by CAIDA), or networks taken in a fixed random order.

    How to read this

    • An incident is a candidate. It is a group of updates that one of our detectors flagged, seen by the peers of one collector: not a verdict on any network (no AS number is published here), and not an incident of the Internet. The types that the detectors do not see are missing from the counts; the table of coverage below says which.
    • Invalid, Unknown, Valid are the verdicts of the downstream ASPA procedure on the AS paths that the incident kept: Invalid when the ASPAs prove the path wrong, Unknown when ASPAs are missing, Valid when the hops attested from the two ends cover the whole path (one lateral hop may sit between them). ASPAs of the day are the ASPAs as they were at the start of the day the incident began (the last state of the day before), replayed from the history of changes. An ASPA judges the AS path, so a wrong origin, flapping and mass withdrawals, which have little or no path, are not expected to be Invalid.
    • Complete ASPAs is the best case: every network publishes the providers that CAIDA infers for it. The valley detector uses the same relationships, so for those incidents the column agrees by construction and measures nothing about ASPA; the ASPAs of the day and the chart are what tells.
    • The time frame is the one under the title: the updates that one collector received between those two times, in UTC. An incident is counted when it has ended (after 15 minutes without updates), so the last minutes of each file are added with the next one. The page is built once a day from the incident files of the last 30 days: with little data the numbers describe a short window, not the Internet.

    The types of incident, which of them the detectors can observe, and how the detectors did on five known incidents are explained in the guide.
