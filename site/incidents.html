    How to read this

    • An incident is a candidate. It is a group of updates that one of our detectors flagged, seen by the peers of one collector: not a verdict on any network (no AS number is published here), and not an incident of the Internet. The types that the detectors do not see are missing from the counts; the table of coverage below says which.
    • Invalid, Unknown, Valid are the verdicts of the downstream ASPA procedure on the AS paths that the incident kept: Invalid when the ASPAs prove the path wrong, Unknown when ASPAs are missing, Valid when every hop is attested. ASPAs of the day are the ASPAs published on the day the incident began, replayed from the history of changes. An ASPA judges the AS path, so a wrong origin, flapping and mass withdrawals, which have little or no path, are not expected to be Invalid.
    • Complete ASPAs is the best case: every network publishes the providers that CAIDA infers for it. The valley detector uses the same relationships, so for those incidents the column agrees by construction and measures nothing about ASPA; the ASPAs of the day and the chart are what tells.
    • The time frame is the one under the title: the updates that one collector received between those two times, in UTC. An incident is counted when it has ended (after 15 minutes without updates), so the last minutes of each file are added with the next one. The page is built once a day from the incident files of the last 30 days: with little data the numbers describe a short window, not the Internet.

    The taxonomy of routing incidents

    These are the types of the taxonomy of our survey of Internet routing security (Sec. III; the reference is at the end of the section), with the acronyms that the survey uses.

    The reliability of what BGP carries depends entirely on the trustworthiness of the peering networks, and BGP has no built-in way to check, while the routes are exchanged, that the information is accurate. Both accidental misconfigurations and malicious actors can compromise inter-AS routing, and such incidents can only be detected afterwards, by other means. The survey harmonises the categories that earlier surveys and RFCs treat either at a very high level or only for a sector of the problem. It distinguishes four macro-categories (route manipulation, routing consistency, policy violation, session-based), each refined into micro-categories and, for some of them, into types, with the same acronyms throughout.

    1. Route manipulation

    Unauthorised modifications of the path or prefix information of BGP, by altering the AS_PATH or by advertising prefixes. They can hijack traffic, impersonate legitimate routes or inject false prefixes, and may lead to interception, blackholing or other security problems.

    Prefix Hijack (PRH). An AS advertises, intentionally or through malfeasance, one or more prefixes that belong to another AS, so that traffic is redirected to the wrong network, with serious consequences for the security and the availability of services. Its types:

    • Complete Hijack (PRH-CH): the attacker announces exactly the prefix of the legitimate AS.
    • Incomplete Hijack (PRH-IH): the attacker announces a more specific prefix than the legitimate ones; BGP selects the longest prefix match, so the traffic to that sub-prefix goes to the hijacker.
    • Interception Hijack (PRH-MIT): the attacker announces a prefix to attract traffic, inspects it and forwards it to the legitimate destination, acting as a man in the middle without visibly disrupting the service.
    • Abusive Hijack (PRH-AB): the attacker announces a prefix that its rightful owner does not currently advertise, often unassigned or unannounced address space. There is no conflicting announcement, so it is harder to detect and can go unnoticed for a long time.

    The subdivision follows the usual hijack classes (exact-prefix, sub-prefix and interception). Documented cases include the 2022 announcement of a prefix of a social network by a Russian provider, and the 2020 announcement of many prefixes of other networks by another Russian provider (survey incidents A1 and A2).

    AS Path Manipulation (ASM). The attacker modifies the AS_PATH of an UPDATE. Its types:

    • AS Path poisoning (ASM-PE): fake ASNs are inserted on purpose. An AS that finds its own number in the path discards the route to avoid a loop, so the attacker can make chosen networks drop a route and move the traffic to another path. It is also used legitimately, for traffic engineering, to avoid an unwanted path.
    • Forged origin Injection (ASM-FI): the path is made to look as if the attacker were directly connected to the target AS, when it is not. Neighbours may prefer the shorter path and send the traffic to the attacker, who can intercept, edit or drop it before forwarding it.
    • Path Shortening (ASM-PS): legitimate ASNs are removed from the path, to make it look shorter and more attractive, and to attract traffic.
    • path EXtension evasion (ASM-EX): the path is inflated with extra ASNs, sometimes fake or private, to penalise a route or make it less likely to be selected, for example to steer the return traffic away from an AS.

    2. Routing consistency

    Attacks on the stability and the integrity of routing, by destabilising the routing tables or exploiting inconsistencies in the information. They cause frequent changes of the routing state, instability, congestion and delayed delivery. The survey places here two well-known operational causes of instability, to connect them to threats and mitigations.

    State Volatility (VOL). When UPDATE messages become unpredictable they can trigger widespread disruptions. The common example is route flapping, a router that advertises and withdraws the same routes again and again, which makes the neighbours reconfigure their tables. It can come from hardware or software instability, from misconfiguration, or from links that go up and down. Its types:

    • Flooding Through Oscillation (VOL-FO): the instability of a physical or logical link triggers a wave of updates and unnecessary churn.
    • Disruptive Flapping (VOL-DF): an attacker induces flapping on purpose, as a denial-of-service vector: the routers recompute routes continuously, consuming CPU and memory, up to congestion or blocking.
    • Amplified Churn Injection (VOL-AC): a local instability leads to widespread updates across the global system: more changes in the tables, more control-plane traffic, instability.
    • Delayed Convergence (VOL-DC): the routing system takes much longer to reach a stable state because of persistent fluctuations, with latency spikes, packet loss and degraded service.

    Prefix Deaggregation (DEG). An AS advertises more specific prefixes instead of aggregated ones. It can be a legitimate operational strategy (finer traffic engineering, different exit points, load balancing), and becomes a problem when abused, intentionally or through negligence, to gain an advantage or to cause harm. Its types:

    • Malicious Deaggregation (DEG-MA): the deliberate announcement of excessively specific prefixes to attract traffic or to stress the routing system.
    • Careless Deaggregation (DEG-CA): overly granular prefixes advertised by operational error or lack of expertise, without malice, which still adds to the instability.
    • Exploitative Deaggregation (DEG-EX): more specific prefixes used for a tactical, if questionable, purpose. For example an ISP with an IPv6 /29 announces up to eight /32 instead of the single route.
    • Fragmentation Flood (DEG-FF): the large-scale version, aimed at exhausting the memory and the processing of the routers. A single /29 can be split into up to 524,288 /48, the smallest commonly accepted IPv6 prefix, against about 219,000 entries in the whole IPv6 table at the time of the survey.

    3. Policy violation

    Attacks that break the routing policies agreed between networks: the unauthorised propagation of routes between ASes that are not meant to exchange them, or the violation of local agreements. They can cause unintended leaks of traffic, misrouting, and the exposure of sensitive information of the network.

    Routing Leaks (RLK). An AS propagates routing advertisements in violation of the intended policies of the sender, of the receiver or of any AS along the path (RFC 7908). Accidental or deliberate, it breaks core principles such as valley-free routing (an AS does not forward the traffic of one peer or provider to another peer or provider). It can cause suboptimal routing, higher latency, congestion, and interception or manipulation of data. A customer must advertise all its networks to its provider, including those of its customers, but not those learned from peers and other providers; a peer must announce only its networks and those of its customers. Its types:

    • Upward Leak (RLK-UL): a customer announces to its peers or providers the routes it learned through transit.
    • Lateral Leak (RLK-LL): a peer propagates routes received from other peers or providers.
    • Downward Leak (RLK-DL): a provider advertises to a customer the routes learned from another provider or peer, which gives the customer unintended transit and makes it a transit between two higher-tier ASes. The survey notes that no well-documented public incident is explicitly classified as RLK-DL, which does not mean that they do not happen.
    • Full Propagation (RLK-FP): the more severe variant: an AS propagates the routes learned from one neighbour to all the others, whatever their role. Documented cases include a 2019 leak of more than 70,000 routes by a Swiss colocation provider to a Chinese carrier, which propagated them across the Internet (A9), and, as an upward leak, a 2025 leak of more than 20,000 routes by an American technology company that lasted about 15 minutes (A10): a longer path can still be preferred, because the path length is only one factor of the selection.

    Policy Manipulation (POL). It exploits the flexibility of the routing policies that each AS defines from local preferences and business relationships: enforced locally, they have global effects on propagation and selection. Its types:

    • AS Path Length Manipulation (POL-PL): announcements that are technically valid but built to make the path look shorter or more attractive, so that others prefer a suboptimal route.
    • Local Preference Manipulation (POL-LP): the attribute that ranks competing routes inside an AS is manipulated, directly or through a misconfiguration in a partner AS, to direct the traffic through a chosen path.
    • MED Abuse (POL-MD): the Multi-Exit Discriminator, which influences the exit chosen between two ASes with several connection points, is altered to influence the inbound traffic, causing congestion or redirection.
    • Selective Propagation (POL-SP): specific prefixes are announced only to a subset of the neighbours, which can attract flows through chosen links or keep routes invisible to monitoring. A reported case uses BGP communities to propagate hijacked routes only to selected upstream providers, to intercept the traffic of targeted regions without widespread visibility (A11).

    4. Session-based

    Attacks on the BGP session itself, the channel through which the messages are exchanged: they can disrupt the normal operation, cause session resets or inject false updates, with loss of routing information and unauthorised changes of behaviour.

    Attribute-Based Session Reset (ATR). They exploit the handling of attributes, when malformed or improperly processed attributes disturb the routing system. The class is linked to the long-discussed error handling of UPDATE messages (RFC 7606) and is presented as a family of its own because of recent large incidents. Its types:

    • Error Handling Policy Abuse (ATR-EH): inconsistent or lax error handling escalates into widespread disruption.
    • Malformed Optional Transitive Attribute Injection (ATR-MA): a malformed attribute is injected and relayed through the network, also by route servers of exchange points that apply no attribute filtering.
    • Vendor-Specific Attribute Exploitation (ATR-VE): the implementations of different vendors react differently to the same attribute: some ignore it as the RFC says, one passes it on, and another resets the sessions instead of discarding the update.

    The documented case is that of 20 May 2025 (A12): an UPDATE with a corrupted Prefix-SID attribute, originated by an AS of the Asia-Pacific region, was handled in different ways by the routers of different vendors and by route servers of exchange points; more than 150,000 updates in 10 seconds followed, with session flaps and instability in several networks. The vendor concerned changed its software to discard such attributes instead of ending the sessions, but the broader challenge of malformed or experimental attributes, especially in shared infrastructure, remains.

    Two families, and who deals with them

    The survey also distinguishes structural or inter-domain policy attacks (PRH, RLK, POL and part of ASM), whose mitigation needs new validation frameworks, shared semantics and coordinated adoption across many networks, from implementation or operational robustness attacks (ATR, VOL, DEG and part of ASM), whose mitigation depends on software robustness, configuration hygiene, monitoring and operational hardening. Academia tends to work first on the former, industry operators on the latter, and institutions become relevant when the countermeasures are mature and the challenge is the coordinated adoption.

    Which types of the taxonomy can we observe

    Not all of them. The table goes through the 27 types of the survey, one by one, and says what the detectors of the project do with each. 5 are detected, 15 only partly (as candidates, or by a related symptom) and 7 cannot be observed from the data that we collect.

    For each type of the taxonomy: whether the detectors of the project observe it and why, and for each class what an ASPA can say
    TypeStatusWhy
    PRH Prefix hijackAn ASPA can say little: it judges the AS path, and the origin is a matter of the ROAs.
    PRH-CH Complete HijackDetectedAn origin that no ROA authorises for a covered prefix. Needs a ROA: a prefix without one is never flagged, and a forged origin that matches the ROA is not seen.
    PRH-IH Incomplete HijackDetectedA more specific prefix longer than the maximum length of the ROA, or announced by another origin. Same limit: only prefixes covered by ROAs.
    PRH-MIT Interception HijackNot observable hereThe interception happens in the data plane and the announcement looks like any other hijack; telling it apart needs traceroutes.
    PRH-AB Abusive HijackPartlyA candidate: a prefix in space that is not for the public Internet, or that the RIRs list as available or reserved. It needs no ROA. Space that no registry lists is not seen.
    ASM AS path manipulationAn ASPA can expose some of the types: a forged origin or a shortened path shows as a hop that the ASPAs do not attest.
    ASM-PE AS Path poisoningPartlyA candidate: an AS that comes back in the path after another one (the shape of poisoning, which routers also drop as a loop), or an AS number that no registry has allocated. Poisoning that does not repeat the announcer is not seen.
    ASM-FI Forged origin InjectionPartlyA candidate: the link between the last AS and the origin is not in the relationships of CAIDA, as if the announcer were next to the prefix owner, and was not in the routing table of the collector at the start of the day either (a link that is already in the table is not forged, whether CAIDA knows it or not: on the first day of data 89% of the candidates were such links). CAIDA lacks real links too, so a candidate is still more often a link that CAIDA does not know than an attack. The ASPA verdict of the incident is what tells them apart.
    ASM-PS Path ShorteningPartlyA candidate: another link of the path that CAIDA does not know and that was not in the table of the collector at the start of the day, as if ASes in between had been removed. Same limit as ASM-FI: most are real links that CAIDA lacks (the table filter took out half of them on the first day).
    ASM-EX path EXtension evasionPartlyA candidate: a private AS number in the path, which should have been removed at the border. Prepending is not flagged: it is ordinary traffic engineering.
    VOL State volatilityAn ASPA says nothing of the flapping; the paths of its announcements are judged like any other.
    VOL-FO Flooding Through OscillationDetectedTen or more announcements and withdrawals of a prefix from one peer within five minutes, seen by three peers of the collector or more in the same window: one incident for the prefix, whatever the number of peers. A prefix that flaps for one peer alone is that peer's session, not the prefix (on the first day, with five flaps and one incident per peer, flapping was 379,000 of 535,000 incidents). The cause of the oscillation is not visible.
    VOL-DF Disruptive FlappingNot observable hereThe difference from VOL-FO is the intent of an attacker, which the updates do not carry.
    VOL-AC Amplified Churn InjectionPartlyA candidate: one origin with 3,000 updates in five minutes, for 300 prefixes or more, seen from 8 peers or more. The thresholds are first guesses.
    VOL-DC Delayed ConvergencePartlyA candidate: a prefix that keeps receiving updates from any peer, with no pause of a minute, for ten minutes or more and 100 updates or more.
    DEG Prefix deaggregationAn ASPA says nothing: the path of a more specific can be perfectly valid.
    DEG-MA Malicious DeaggregationPartlySeen only when the more specific has another origin than the aggregate, and no ROA authorises that origin for it, and CAIDA knows no link between the two origins: a customer announcing its part of the provider's block is the ordinary use of address space, not a candidate (on the first day, three quarters of them). The intent is not visible.
    DEG-CA Careless DeaggregationPartlySeen as excessive deaggregation, the line DEG-XS of the table by type: 32 or more more specifics of one origin under a /16 (IPv4) or a /32 (IPv6) in a day, from 2 peers or more, or a jump of an origin against its days before. The intent is not visible, so DEG-CA and DEG-EX share that line.
    DEG-EX Exploitative DeaggregationPartlyThe same line as DEG-CA, DEG-XS: the count of the more specifics tells how many, not why.
    DEG-FF Fragmentation FloodPartlyA candidate: 1,024 more specifics or more under a /32 (IPv6), or 1,000 IPv4 more specifics of one origin, in a day. The thresholds are first guesses.
    RLK Routing leakThis is what an ASPA proves: a path with a valley is Invalid.
    RLK-UL Upward LeakDetectedA hop that learned the route from a provider or a peer and sent it to a provider, by the relationships of CAIDA. The incident records the type (incident.py, rlk_type).
    RLK-LL Lateral LeakDetectedThe same detector, when the route is sent to a peer: our reading of the survey is that an upward leak goes to a provider and a lateral one to a peer.
    RLK-DL Downward LeakNot observable hereA leak towards a customer shows in the path as an upward leak of that customer, so the detector flags the valley and cannot tell which of the two it is: that needs the policy of the sender.
    RLK-FP Full PropagationPartlyA proxy: one AS that leaks to ten or more different neighbours within one hour, an event, as the known cases were. The survey means all the neighbours, whatever their role, which needs the policy of that AS; the proxy is set when the page is built, over all the incidents. An AS whose leaks begin in twelve different hours or more of the period is in the valley all day: that is a relationship CAIDA gets wrong (an open peering policy read as peering), not a leak, and its incidents are a line of their own, persistent valley, outside the three types.
    POL Policy manipulationAn ASPA says nothing.
    POL-PL AS Path Length ManipulationNot observable hereA path that is valid but built to look shorter cannot be told from a real one.
    POL-LP Local Preference ManipulationNot observable hereThe attribute stays inside the AS and the collector does not see it.
    POL-MD MED AbuseNot observable hereThe scripts keep only prefix, peer, origin and path; the MED is not recorded, and a change of exit is not an anomaly in itself.
    POL-SP Selective PropagationNot observable hereA route that is announced to a few neighbours only is, by definition, missing at the collector.
    ATR Attribute-based session resetAn ASPA says nothing.
    ATR-EH Error Handling Policy AbusePartlyThe session of a peer that ends with a NOTIFICATION for an UPDATE message error (RFC 7606 asks to treat such an update as withdrawn instead), and the mass withdrawal of a peer (more than 500 in 10 seconds), which an ordinary reset looks like. Only the sessions of the peers with the collector.
    ATR-MA Malformed Optional Transitive Attribute InjectionPartlyThe same NOTIFICATION when the error is in an attribute (list, flags, length or optional attribute). The malformed attribute itself is not kept.
    ATR-VE Vendor-Specific Attribute ExploitationPartlySame: the vendor and the attribute are not visible.
    • Detected: a detector flags this behaviour. Partly: it flags a candidate, a related symptom, or only some of the cases; a candidate is not a verdict, and the thresholds that decide it are first guesses. Not observable here: the updates of a collector do not carry it, and it would need other data (traceroutes, the configuration of the AS, a view from inside the network).
    • One window on the Internet. The updates come from the peers of a single collector (RIPE RIS rrc00), and only the updates, not the tables. An incident that stays out of the best routes of those peers is not seen at all, and one that began before the capture is seen only from the moment it changes. The cases that a collector cannot see are studied after the fact, from reports.
